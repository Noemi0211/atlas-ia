## The legal challenge of the 21st century

Generative AI has broken the traditional frameworks of intellectual property. If an AI creates an image, a text or a song, who is the author? The user who wrote the prompt? The developers of the model? The creators of the training data?

<Callout type="info">
We are in a moment of legal transition. Intellectual property frameworks were designed for a world without generative AI. Courts and legislators around the world are defining new rules on the fly.
</Callout>

## Can an AI be an author?

Currently, most jurisdictions (US, EU, UK) hold that only humans can be authors. A work generated entirely by AI without significant human intervention cannot be registered with copyright.

### Human intervention

The key factor is the degree of human intervention. The US Copyright Office distinguishes between:

- **AI as a tool:** the human creatively directs the AI (e.g., Photoshop with AI)
- **AI as a creator:** the AI generates without significant human control (e.g., "draw a cat")

In the first case, the work may have copyright. In the second, it may not.

## Training and copyright

### The debate

Huge amounts of data extracted from the internet, including copyrighted works, are used to train models such as GPT-4, DALL-E or Midjourney. Is this fair use or infringement?

### Positions

- **AI companies:** argue that it is fair use, similar to how a human learns from existing works
- **Creators and publishers:** argue that it is mass infringement and demand compensation
- **Governments:** looking for a balance between innovation and rights

### Landmark lawsuits

- *The New York Times vs. OpenAI:* lawsuit over the use of articles to train models
- *Getty Images vs. Stability AI:* over the use of images without a licence
- *Authors vs. OpenAI:* writers claiming compensation for their works used in training

<Callout type="warning">
The legal landscape changes rapidly. What seems acceptable today may be illegal tomorrow. If you work with AI-generated content in professional contexts, consult a specialised legal advisor.
</Callout>

## Licences and attribution

### Models with different licences

| Model | Usage licence | Generated content is... |
|-------|---------------|-------------------------|
| DALL-E 3 | Private | Owned by the user |
| Midjourney | Private | Owned by the user (paid plan) |
| Stable Diffusion | Open (research) | No known restrictions |
| Claude / ChatGPT | Private | Owned by the user |
| Gemini | Private | Owned by the user |

### Good practices

- **Always attribute** the use of AI when it is relevant
- **Check the licence** of the tool you use
- **Do not assume** that generated content is royalty-free
- **Document** the creation process to evidence human authorship

## Implications for educators

### In the classroom

- AI-generated content must be labelled as such
- Students must learn to cite and attribute correctly
- Originality policies must be updated for the age of AI
- The debate about AI plagiarism requires nuance

### For educational materials

- Materials created with AI for educational use may have restrictions
- Educational platforms must specify their AI policies
- Attribution is good practice even when it is not legally required

## The future of intellectual property

### Possible scenarios

1. **Reformed copyright:** new frameworks that recognise human-AI collaboration
2. **Mandatory licensing:** automatic compensation systems for creators
3. **Expanded public domain:** AI-generated works without automatic protection
4. **Mandatory watermarks:** digital identification of AI-generated content

<Callout type="tip">
Regardless of what the courts decide, transparency is always the best policy. Declare the use of AI in your creations, respect licences and attribute when appropriate.
</Callout>
