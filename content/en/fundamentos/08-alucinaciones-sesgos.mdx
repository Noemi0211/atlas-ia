## Hallucinations, biases and limitations

This is probably **the most important lesson** of the whole Block 1.

If you only remember something about AI's limitations, remember this: **AI can seem very intelligent and be completely wrong**.

## What is a hallucination?

A hallucination is when an AI model **generates information that seems plausible but is completely false**.

### Real example

Ask ChatGPT:

> Who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2024?

It is possible that it gives you a name with total confidence, and that the name is **completely invented**.

### Why does it happen?

LLMs do not "understand" information. They predict the **most likely next word** based on statistical patterns.

If the pattern says that after "Nobel Prize in Literature" comes a famous writer's name, the model will produce a name — even if it is the wrong one.

### Common examples of hallucinations

- **Invented data**: Dates, statistics, figures that seem real but are not
- **False references**: Scientific articles that do not exist, books never written
- **Invented quotes**: Attributing phrases to people who never said them
- **Nonexistent places**: Restaurants, streets, buildings that do not exist

<Callout type="error">
Hallucinations are not random errors. They are a **fundamental feature** of how LLMs work. They cannot "stop" hallucinating because hallucination and text generation are the same process.
</Callout>

## What are biases?

Biases are **systematic prejudices** in the model's responses, derived from the training data.

### Types of biases

- **Cultural bias**: The model predominantly reflects Western/Anglo-Saxon culture
- **Gender bias**: It can associate certain jobs with certain genders
- **Temporal bias**: Its training data has a cutoff date
- **Popularity bias**: It tends to support majority opinions

### Example

If you ask "describe a doctor", the model may automatically assume it is a man. If you ask "describe a nurse", it may assume it is a woman.

This reflects the biases of the training data, not reality.

## Fundamental limitations

### It has no access to real-time information

A pure LLM does not know what happened yesterday. It has no internet. It only knows what was in its training data.

### It cannot verify its own responses

It has no internal mechanism to check whether what it says is true. It simply generates what seems most likely.

### It has no real understanding

Although it seems to understand, it is actually manipulating statistical patterns. It has no experience of the real world.

### It does not reason like a human

It cannot:
- Think critically in a genuine way
- Understand the emotional context of a situation
- Learn from a conversation in real time
- Distinguish between fact and opinion

### It can be manipulated

With the right prompts, a model can be made to say things it should not say or to generate problematic content.

## How to protect yourself

### Always verify

For critical data, **never rely** only on AI. Verify with primary sources.

### Double-check with multiple sources

If AI gives you a piece of data, look it up on Google, Wikipedia or an official source.

### Be skeptical of numbers

Statistical data, dates and figures are where hallucinations are most dangerous.

### Use AI as a starting point

AI is excellent for:
- Generating initial ideas
- Structuring information
- Exploring different perspectives
- Making drafts

**It is not** excellent for:
- Exact factual data
- Legal or medical information
- Quotes and references
- Critical decisions

<Callout type="tip">
A good habit: when AI gives you an important piece of data, ask it to state its confidence level. Then verify. This forces you to be critical instead of passive.
</Callout>

## The confidence paradox

AI systems generate responses with **total confidence**, even when they are wrong. They do not say "I am not sure" or "I could be wrong".

This apparent confidence is dangerous because humans tend to trust those who speak with confidence.

**Remember**: confidence in the answer ≠ certainty that it is correct.
